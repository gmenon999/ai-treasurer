Your AI cash forecast just refreshed. Can Treasury prove what it knew last Monday?

Why every major Treasury decision needs a forecast version of record, and what that record should hold.

By Gopakumar Menon, CA, CPA, CTP ·17 November 2026 ·3 min read

At a glance

When AI produces the cash forecast, the view you acted on can be replaced as soon as new data arrives. For every major decision, keep a forecast version of record.

What to keep:

  • The snapshot
  • The inputs
  • The parameters
  • The rationale

Your AI cash forecast just refreshed. Now the Board is asking why you did not draw on the revolving credit facility last Monday. Can you prove what the forecast said on that day, or has the automatic refresh overwritten it?

Illustrative example

At 9am on Monday, the cash forecast showed a large customer receipt due on Thursday. Based on that view, Treasury decided not to draw on the credit facility. Thursday comes, and the money does not arrive.

A week later, the Board asks: why did we not borrow? Treasury opens the forecast to show what it said on Monday, and it can’t. The AI tool has recalculated the forecast many times since, and it now shows the receipt as delayed.

Why this matters more with AI

Many AI forecasting tools refresh the forecast constantly as new data arrives, and not all of them save earlier versions. Without that point in time view, past Treasury decisions get judged on hindsight rather than on the information the team actually had.

A sound decision can look poor, and a poor one that happened to work out can look sound.

What to keep for each major decision

For every major decision, keep a forecast version of record:

  1. The snapshot: the exact forecast shown at approval, saved as a copy that cannot be edited
  2. The inputs: data sources and when each was updated
  3. The parameters: underlying assumptions and AI model version, where the provider gives one
  4. The rationale: who decided and why

Make saving this record a mandatory part of your workflow, so no major decision can be approved without it. Name who keeps the record, and keep it for as long as your policy keeps other approval evidence. Created at approval, the record is quick to make. Rebuilt weeks later, it may be impossible.

Judge a decision by what was known when it was made, not by what is known now.

What the record is for

  • Defending the decision. The Board and the auditor can judge whether it was reasonable on the evidence available at the time.
  • Finding the cause. When an outcome turns out badly, the record shows why. For example:
    • The forecast was wrong
    • A data source was out of date
    • The decision went against the forecast
    • No one could have known at the time
    Each cause needs a different response.
  • Measuring the AI. Compare actual cash with what the forecast said on the day each decision was made, not with today’s version. That is the accuracy that matters for decisions.

Which decisions need a record

Not every transaction needs a record. Set a clear materiality threshold in your Treasury policy, and keep a record for every decision above it, such as drawing on a facility, placing a deposit, entering a hedge or releasing a major payment.

Examples only, not a full list.

Try this

Take last month’s largest funding decision. Can you pull up the forecast exactly as it stood when you approved it? If you can’t, start keeping a record from the next decision.

And ask your AI tool provider: does it keep earlier versions of the forecast, or show only the latest numbers?

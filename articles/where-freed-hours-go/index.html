Your reconciliation only takes a few minutes. So why is your team still busy?

How to plan the time automation frees up, and how to check where it went.

By Gopakumar Menon, CA, CPA, CTP ·18 November 2026 ·3 min read

Illustrative example

A Treasury team automates its daily bank reconciliation.

Matching that took most of the morning now takes minutes.

Six months later, the CFO asks one question.

Where did the hours go?

No one can answer.

The tool worked. No one decided what the saved time was for.

It makes no difference whether the tool is AI, robotic process automation or simple matching rules. The question is the same.

Saved hours and freed people are not the same thing

Automation does not hand you back a person.

It hands you back pieces of time.

An hour for one analyst. Half an hour for another.

No one plans what to do with half an hour.

So it goes to whatever is nearest.

The analyst does not trust the tool yet. She checks every match again, by hand, with no end date.

The manager adds another tab to the daily cash report.

The weekly meeting runs longer, because now there is room for it.

Six months on, the team is just as busy.

Nothing new has been done.

The hours were not wasted. They were absorbed.

Busy is one problem. Idle is another.

Sometimes the work does not get absorbed. It simply disappears.

The analyst who ran the reconciliation now has empty mornings.

No new task. No conversation about what comes next.

Everyone can see it. Including the analyst.

Idle time does not stay neutral. The analyst wonders whether the role is next. Some people invent work to look busy. Others quietly switch off.

Either way, the business pays for capacity and gets neither the saving nor the new work.

The fix is simple, and easy to skip. Tell the person, before go live, what their time will move to.

If the new work is not ready, give the gap a purpose and an end date:

  • Check the tool’s matches against the bank statement for a set period, then stop
  • Train for the work that is coming
  • Learn a colleague’s role, so the team has cover
  • Write down how the new process works, for the auditor

Plan the work before the time arrives

Ask three questions before go live.

  1. Whose time comes back? Map it by person. A few hours spread across five people is not a role.
  2. What will it become? Bundle the pieces into named work with an owner. A weekly liquidity forecast. A second check on large payments.
  3. Where is it going? New work, growth without new hires, a stronger control, or a cost saving.

A cost saving is a fair choice. Just call it a saving, not transformation.

Then check where it went

Record the hours before go live. Six months later, explain the difference.

Hours turned into a cost saving: saved.
Hours moved to planned work: reinvested.
Hours you cannot explain: lost.

And plan the training with the automation. Matching transactions and building a forecast are not the same skill.

The question for the Board

The debate is whether AI and automation take Finance jobs.

The more useful question is smaller.

Who decided what the saved hours are for?

If the answer is no one, they have already been spent.

Try this

Take one automated process. Write four columns: hours before, hours after, where the time was planned to go, where it went. If the last two do not match, you have found where the hours went.

P.S. If your CFO asked tomorrow where last year’s automation hours went, who on your team could answer?
